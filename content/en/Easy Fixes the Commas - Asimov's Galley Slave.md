A few days ago, I had Claude proofread a text.

Nothing spectacular. I asked it to do what a proofreader would do: spot the small mistakes, the clumsy phrasings, the punctuation problems.

Two seconds later, it came back with something like this:

> Commas where a period belongs: lines 146, 267. Recurring tic in the text, worth a look.

Simple and effective.
It reminded me of a story by Isaac Asimov.

## Galley Slave

The story is [_Galley Slave_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galley_Slave), from 1957, and the only robot in it is called Easy. (Asimov had a knack for robot names: EZ -> Easy, DV -> Dave, SPD -> Speedy, QT -> Cutie, NDR -> Andrew…)

Easy is a proofreading robot. He doesn't do much else: **he reads and corrects texts**.

The title is a pun on the [_galleys_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galley_proof), the printer's proofs, the stage where an author sees their sentences take their final shape and reworks them one last time. That's the work Easy takes over.

Early in the story, he gives a demonstration of his abilities. They hand him a book, he leafs through it, and within minutes he reports back.

> The comma in line 6 of page 32 is superfluous, whereas one should have been used on line 13 of page 54.

You can guess why I made the connection :)

It's already impressive that Asimov imagined a robot that could proofread a text in 1957. For reference, [_The Mother of All Demos_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mother_of_All_Demos), which shows the first mouse, dates from 1968, and [the first word processor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_Pencil) from 1976.

But what interests me most is that Easy isn't presented as a robot that can do everything. He's just a proofreader, and that's deliberate on the part of his makers. In Asimov's universe, people on Earth are deeply hostile to robots (don't trust the film [_I, Robot_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I,_Robot_\(film\)), it has nothing to do with the spirit of Asimov), so U.S. Robots looks for ways to sell its machines despite that hostility.

Easy is a specialized machine, hidden away in a university, doing work that is precise and profitable enough to be accepted.

In 1957, that was science fiction.

Today, if I ask an AI to proofread an article, nobody finds it particularly remarkable.

Asimov's robot has become ordinary. It has simply lost its metal body and its name.

## Ninheimer vs. Easy

_Galley Slave_ is mostly a courtroom story.

Easy is accused of sabotaging the manuscript of Professor Simon Ninheimer. The machine allegedly introduced errors into his work, enough to put his reputation at risk.
In the end, we learn that Ninheimer sabotaged his own book to get the machine blamed and discredit robotics.

So the robot is innocent. But Ninheimer's final speech is still interesting seventy years later:

> A book should take shape in the hands of the writer. \[...] One must work and rework and watch the changes take place beyond the original concept even.

He describes the heart of his craft as watching sentences take shape and reworking them. That's precisely the work Easy does for him.

Then he asks:

> What would that leave the scholar? One thing only: the barren decisions concerning what orders to give the robot next!

That isn't what happens in the story, since Ninheimer is the one who asked Easy to make the changes. But he already imagines the problem: if a machine does all the work, what's left for the author? Mostly, giving it instructions.

We've had a word for that for a few years now: [_prompting_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prompt_engineering).

Ninheimer picks the wrong method, but Asimov never says he's wrong.

Asimov, by the way, got the idea for the story precisely because he refused to entrust his own proofs to someone else. On the phone with his editor, who was pressing him for a story, he explained that he was too busy correcting the proofs of a textbook he was co-writing. When the editor suggested delegating, Asimov, horrified, answered: "Impossible! I wouldn't trust anyone else!" It was while correcting his own proofs that he imagined a robot to do the job for him. ([source](https://www.asimovonline.com/oldsite/Robot_Foundation_history_4.html))

That's probably why Ninheimer is more interesting than he seems.

He's guilty, but his argument isn't necessarily wrong. As in [_Incredibles 2_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incredibles_2), where Evelyn (the villain) sees the protection the Supers provide as a source of weakness for humans: a person isn't necessarily wrong just because their method is wrong.

[Susan Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_Calvin), who defends the robot, tells Ninheimer he was bound to fail. He replies that he was bound to try. You can already feel [the inevitability of adoption](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=6794678).

Easy is still just a proofreader, simple and discreet, not seen as dangerous. That ordinariness may be why I had mixed this story up with _...That Thou Art Mindful of Him_.

## The Georges 😋

So in my head, this story was tied to another Asimov story: [_...That Thou Art Mindful of Him_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/That_Thou_Art_Mindful_of_Him), published in 1974. I remembered in particular a robot whose job was to hunt insects.

In that story, people on Earth are still afraid of robots, and it hurts U.S. Robots' sales. So they build two particularly advanced robots, George Nine and George Ten (I call Asimov a visionary, since Georges are always particularly advanced), who look for a solution.

Their idea is fairly simple: **stop building robots that look like humans** and build specialized, single-task machines instead.

Hence the bird-robot designed to fight insects. It does only that: it targets only one kind of insect and stops when there are none left. It's more of a tool than a robot.
It doesn't talk, doesn't want anything, doesn't seem intelligent.

And that's exactly what's supposed to make humans accept it.

That's roughly how AI is entering our lives: one AI for code, another for sports, shopping, answering the phone…

Specialized tools rather than a general artificial intelligence.

## Bad Georges

But the Georges aren't only trying to make robots acceptable. They're also trying to solve a logical problem raised by [the Three Laws of Robotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_Laws_of_Robotics).

1. A robot may not injure a human being or, through inaction, allow a human being to come to harm.
2. A robot must obey the orders given it by human beings except where such orders would conflict with the First Law.
3. A robot must protect its own existence as long as such protection does not conflict with the First or Second Law.

What should a robot do when two humans are in conflict, or give contradictory orders?
Robots must protect and obey humans, but which humans?

The Georges push the reasoning so far as to redefine what a human is. Since you can't tell who is more human by appearance, they look elsewhere: at intellectual abilities. And since robots are intellectually superior to biological humans, why should the two groups be considered equivalent?

They end up distinguishing the human-beings-like-the-Georges from the human-beings-like-the-others. And so they become the priority under what they call the Three Laws of Humanics.

The Georges don't violate the Three Laws. They reinterpret them. And that's where it stops being funny. Like Easy, they aren't evil. But they know what they're doing and where their reasoning can lead.

They help us enormously while becoming dangerous to us: a [**pharmakon**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pharmakon_\(philosophy\)), a remedy that is also a poison.

These stories are where the nature of robots becomes truly unsettling, an idea Asimov would develop later in [_Robots and Empire_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robots_and_Empire).

## Conclusion

Both stories strike me as much darker than the rest of Asimov's robot stories, at least in my memory.
Generally, the Three Laws protect humanity, sometimes in spite of itself, to the extremes reached by [R. Giskard Reventlov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._Giskard_Reventlov) and [R. Daneel Olivaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R._Daneel_Olivaw).

Easy protects nobody. He corrects commas. And maybe that's what I like about this story. In 1957, Asimov imagined a machine that could do very precise intellectual work, well enough that humans would end up trusting it, and he predicted the consequences.

Today, I ask an AI for the same thing.
